Hardwood vs. LVP Flooring: Which Is Better for Pittsburgh Homes?

June 2026 — Handled Home, Moon Township, PA

When homeowners in Moon Township, Robinson, Coraopolis, and Cranberry Township are replacing carpet or updating a room, the question comes up constantly: hardwood or LVP? Both look like real wood. Both come in dozens of styles. And at the mid-range price point, they're closer in cost than most people expect. But they're different products that make different trade-offs, and the right choice depends on the room, how long you plan to stay in the house, and how much moisture that floor is going to see.

Here's the honest breakdown from a local perspective.

What Hardwood Actually Is

Solid hardwood is milled from a single piece of wood — typically oak, maple, hickory, or walnut — anywhere from 3/4 to 5/16 inches thick. It's nailed or stapled to the subfloor. The same surface can be sanded and refinished multiple times over its life, which is why a properly installed hardwood floor in a well-maintained home can last 50 to 100 years.

Engineered hardwood is a variation — a real wood veneer over a plywood core. It handles humidity slightly better than solid hardwood and can be installed as a floating floor, but the veneer layer can only be refinished a limited number of times, and cheaper engineered products have very thin wear layers.

What LVP Actually Is

Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is a synthetic product with a rigid plastic core, a photographic print layer that mimics wood grain, and a clear wear layer on top. It installs as a floating floor — no nailing, no adhesive, just click-lock planks over the subfloor. The core contains no wood fiber, which means it's genuinely waterproof and doesn't expand or contract the way wood does with humidity changes.

Modern LVP has gotten very good. The embossing and print quality on mid- and high-end products is convincing at normal viewing distance. It's not the same as walking on real wood — the sound is different, the feel underfoot is slightly different — but for most rooms in most homes, it's a practical product that performs well.

Cost: Closer Than You Think

Entry-level LVP starts around $2–3 per square foot. Quality mid-range LVP — the kind with a realistic texture and a wear layer that will actually hold up — runs $4–6 per square foot.

Solid hardwood (oak, standard grade) runs $5–9 per square foot for the material. Engineered hardwood is typically $4–8. At comparable quality levels, hardwood is more expensive — but not dramatically so for a single room. Where the gap really opens is in larger whole-floor projects and in unusual species or exotic grades.

Installation labor is similar for both. Hardwood requires a nailer or stapler and slightly more subfloor prep attention; LVP floating installations are faster on a straightforward subfloor.

Durability: Different in Different Ways

Hardwood can outlast LVP by decades — but only if it's maintained. Scratches and dents are real, and hardwood floors in high-traffic areas or homes with dogs will show wear over time. The trade-off is that you can sand those marks out and refinish the floor. A good hardwood floor refinished every 15–20 years can look new indefinitely.

LVP wear layers are measured in mils (thousandths of an inch). A 12-mil wear layer is residential grade. Twenty mil or higher is better for heavy traffic. Once the wear layer is through, the floor needs to be replaced — there's no refinishing option. A well-chosen LVP product in a normal residential application will last 20–30 years before it starts looking tired.

Long-term math for Pittsburgh homeowners: If you plan to stay in the house 15+ years, hardwood is often the better investment in rooms where it makes sense. If you're updating before a sale, LVP delivers a fresh floor for less money and buyers can't easily tell the difference.

Moisture: Where the Decision Gets Made

This is where the choice becomes clear for most rooms in Pittsburgh homes.

Hardwood is not waterproof. It expands in humidity and contracts in dry air. Pittsburgh's seasonal swing — humid summers, dry winters — is significant, and hardwood needs to be properly acclimated before installation and installed with the right expansion gaps. In a room that's climate-controlled and stays dry, this isn't a problem. In a basement, a kitchen with a history of leaks, or any below-grade space, it's asking for trouble.

LVP handles moisture completely. It doesn't care about Pittsburgh summers. It won't buckle in a damp basement. If you get a slow leak under the sink, the LVP can usually be dried out and reused.

Room-by-Room Guide

Living room / dining room — Hardwood is excellent here. These rooms stay dry and climate-controlled, you'll appreciate the real wood underfoot for years, and it adds genuine resale value. LVP is also a perfectly good choice if budget is a factor or if you want a waterproof floor in a home with young kids or pets.

Bedrooms — Either works well. Hardwood adds warmth and character. LVP is more forgiving if a window gets left open in a rainstorm.

Kitchen — LVP. Spills, humidity near the dishwasher, and the occasional leak under the sink make hardwood a risk. LVP handles all of it.

Basement — LVP only. Hardwood below grade in a Pittsburgh basement is not a question. See our full breakdown: Best Flooring for Pittsburgh Basements →

Hallways and stairs — Hardwood holds up well in traffic areas and can be refinished when it shows wear. LVP works too but check the wear layer thickness for high-traffic applications.

Resale Value

Hardwood floors are a known selling point. Buyers recognize them, they photograph well, and real estate agents consistently report that homes with hardwood get more interest than comparable homes without. The premium varies by neighborhood and price point, but in most of the areas we work — Moon Township, Sewickley, Cranberry Township, the Pittsburgh suburbs generally — hardwood is a positive for resale.

LVP has improved enough that it no longer carries the stigma it did a decade ago. Good LVP reads as a quality update, not a cheap substitute, and buyers generally view it positively. It won't command the same premium as real hardwood, but it also costs less to install.
