Leaking Faucet FAQs

Can a leaking faucet be repaired without full replacement?

Usually yes. Most faucet leaks come from a worn cartridge, O-ring, washer, or packing nut — parts that cost a few dollars and take 30–45 minutes to swap. We always try repair first. If the faucet body itself is cracked or the parts aren't available, we'll let you know it's time to replace.

Do I need to turn off my water before you arrive?

No — we handle that. We shut off the supply at the valve under the sink or at the main if needed. Just make sure we can access the area under the sink (clear out any items stored there if possible).

What causes a faucet to start leaking?

The most common causes are worn cartridges or ceramic discs (single-handle faucets), deteriorated washers or O-rings (older compression faucets), and loose or corroded supply line connections. High water pressure can also accelerate wear over time.

How much does a leaking faucet repair cost?

Most faucet leaks are quick repairs. We charge by the job, not the hour — contact us for a free estimate based on what you're seeing. If we determine a replacement is more cost-effective, we'll tell you upfront.

Leaking Faucet Repair in Moon Township and Pittsburgh Area Homes

A leaking faucet isn't just an annoyance — it adds up. A faucet dripping once per second wastes more than 3,000 gallons of water per year, and Pittsburgh Water and Sewer Authority bills accordingly. More practically, a faucet that drips constantly eventually leads to mineral staining, damaged cabinet finishes from moisture, and corrosion at the supply connections that makes the eventual repair harder and more expensive. Catching a dripping faucet early is always the better outcome.

Leaking faucet calls in Moon Township homes tend to come from two sources: older single-handle or two-handle faucets where the cartridge or seats and springs have worn out after years of use, and compression faucets in older homes that predate modern cartridge designs. In both cases, the repair is usually a parts replacement — cartridge, O-rings, or seats and springs — rather than a full faucet swap, though we let you know if the faucet has reached the point where repair doesn't make economic sense and replacement is the better value.

When we handle a leaking faucet, we start by identifying exactly where the leak is coming from — spout drip, handle seep, base leak, or supply connection — because the diagnosis changes the fix. We shut off the supply, disassemble the faucet, replace the failed parts, and reassemble and test under pressure. If we find anything during the repair that suggests the faucet won't hold long even after the fix — internal corrosion, damage to the valve seat — we tell you so you can make an informed call about repair versus replacement.
