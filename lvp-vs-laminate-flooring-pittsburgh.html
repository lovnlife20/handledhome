LVP vs. Laminate Flooring: Which Is Right for Your Pittsburgh Home?

June 2026 — Handled Home, Moon Township, PA

Walk into any flooring store or home improvement warehouse and you'll find LVP and laminate sitting next to each other, looking almost identical, priced within a few dollars per square foot of each other. Both are floating floors. Both click together without adhesive. Both can pass for real wood at a glance. And both are popular replacements for the worn carpet and outdated vinyl in homes across Moon Township, Robinson, Coraopolis, and Cranberry Township.

The difference between them isn't how they look — it's how they handle water, humidity, and the kind of climate Pittsburgh actually has. Getting that decision right matters more here than it does in most places.

What LVP Actually Is

Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is a 100% synthetic product. The core is plastic — usually a rigid composite or stone polymer composite (SPC) — with a photographic wood-look layer on top protected by a clear wear layer. Because the core contains no wood fiber or paper, it doesn't absorb moisture. It doesn't swell. It doesn't warp. It can sit in a wet environment for an extended period and come out intact.

That waterproof core is why LVP has largely replaced laminate as the default floating floor choice in most of the rooms we work in.

What Laminate Actually Is

Laminate has a core made of high-density fiberboard (HDF) — compressed wood fiber. The photographic layer on top looks nearly identical to LVP, but the core underneath is fundamentally different. Wood fiber absorbs moisture. When it gets wet and dries repeatedly, it swells and the joints start to separate. One significant moisture event — a sump pump failure, a slow seep after a heavy rain, a spill that isn't cleaned up quickly — can ruin laminate flooring permanently.

That doesn't make laminate a bad product. It makes it the wrong product for the wrong room.

The Pittsburgh Factor

Pittsburgh has one of the wider seasonal humidity swings in the Northeast. Summers are humid — July and August regularly see relative humidity above 80%. Winters are dry. That swing causes wood-based materials to expand and contract, and laminate without proper expansion gaps is one of the most common buckling problems we see in homes in Moon Township and the surrounding area.

Add to that the fact that a lot of homes in the area have basements that run damp in summer, and older construction with foundations that let moisture in slowly, and you have a climate where the waterproof core of LVP is a genuine advantage — not just a marketing feature.

Rule of thumb for Pittsburgh homes: If there's any chance the room gets humid in summer, or if it's below grade at all, choose LVP. Laminate is only the right call in rooms that stay consistently dry and climate-controlled year-round.

Room-by-Room Guide

Kitchens — LVP. Spills, steam, humidity near the sink, and moisture from appliances make this a clear choice. Laminate in a kitchen is a risk that eventually pays out.

Bathrooms — LVP if you want a floating floor. For full bathroom floor tile, that's a different trade — we don't install tile, so if that's the direction you're going you'll need a tile contractor. But if you want a floating floor in a bathroom, LVP is the only one that makes sense.

Basements — LVP, always. Pittsburgh basements are not a debate. See our full breakdown: Best Flooring for Pittsburgh Basements →

Living rooms and dining rooms — Either works if the room stays dry. LVP is still the safer call if the house runs humid in summer. Laminate is fine if you're confident in the room's conditions and want a slightly more solid, authentic feel underfoot.

Bedrooms — Either works. Bedrooms stay dry and climate-controlled, which is exactly where laminate performs well. LVP is still a perfectly good choice here; it just isn't the only reasonable one.

Home offices — Either. Treat it like a bedroom — if it's above grade and stays dry, laminate is fine. If it's a finished basement office, LVP.

Cost Difference

LVP and laminate are close in price at the mid-range. Entry-level laminate can be cheaper, but the quality range is wide and lower-end laminate installed in the wrong conditions fails faster than good-quality LVP would. At similar quality levels, expect LVP to run maybe $0.50–$1.50 more per square foot depending on the product. For most rooms, that's not a significant difference against the total cost of installation.

The more important cost calculation: what's the cost of replacing the floor if you choose laminate and it buckles? That's the same job twice. LVP's upfront premium is cheap insurance against that outcome.

What About Hardwood?

LVP and laminate aren't the only options. If you're looking at dry above-grade rooms — living rooms, dining rooms, bedrooms — hardwood is worth considering. It's more expensive and not waterproof, but it can be refinished repeatedly, lasts decades, and adds genuine resale value. We've written a full comparison: Hardwood vs. LVP for Pittsburgh Homes →
