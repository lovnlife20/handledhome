The Doors in Robinson Homes Are All About the Same Age

Robinson Township's residential building boom ran from the mid-1990s through the mid-2000s, and the interior finish package was consistent across most of it: hollow-core interior doors, bifold doors on the closets, colonial or ranch casing, and MDF baseboard. Practical, affordable, and standard for the time.

Two and a half decades in, that package shows its age in specific and predictable ways. Hollow-core doors sag slightly at the latch side as their hinges take on years of load. Bifold hardware — the top pivot and the track guide — wears until the door no longer runs true and jumps off. MDF baseboard swells at the bottom edge anywhere it's met moisture, particularly in laundry rooms and near exterior doors. And casing that's been bumped, scuffed, and repainted repeatedly starts to look tired even when it's structurally fine.

None of this is dramatic. It's just the accumulation that makes an otherwise well-kept house feel worn. It's also very fixable, and it's the kind of work where doing several items in one visit costs far less than handling them one at a time.

Door Problems Specific to Robinson Homes

Doors That Only Stick in Summer

This is seasonal, and it's one of the most common calls we get across Robinson between June and September. Wood absorbs moisture from humid air and expands; the door grows just enough to bind against the jamb, then shrinks back and works fine again in October. The mistake is planing a door down during its swollen summer size — come winter you're left with a visible gap that never closes properly. We check the hinge side first and adjust there where we can, and only remove material when the fit is genuinely wrong year-round. Read more: Why Doors Stick in Summer →

Bifold Doors on Builder Hardware

Nearly every closet in Robinson's 1990s and 2000s housing got bifold doors on the builder's standard hardware, and that hardware is the weak point. The top pivot wears oval, the track guide loses its grip, and eventually the door comes off the track when you open it. Replacing the pivots and guides — rather than just lifting the door back on — is what actually fixes it. If several closets in your house have the same problem, and they usually do, doing them all in one visit is the sensible approach.

Garage Entry & Exterior Door Weatherstripping

The weatherstripping and door sweeps on exterior doors have a service life well under twenty-five years, so on original Robinson doors it's almost always compressed flat or cracked. The tell is a visible light gap at the bottom or a draft you can feel in January. That's an easy, high-value fix in a climate with our winters. We also handle related caulking and weatherproofing at the same time.

Townhome Doors and Shared Walls

The townhomes along the Route 60 corridor put more doors into a smaller footprint — and often on walls shared with a neighbor, where a door that slams reads through to the other unit. Adjusting the closer, adding a proper stop, and getting the latch to catch cleanly makes a real difference in a shared-wall home. It's a small job with an outsized quality-of-life payoff.

Robinson Township Door & Trim FAQs

My door only sticks in humid weather. Should I plane it?

Usually not, and this is worth getting right. A door that fits fine in winter and binds in August is responding to seasonal moisture, not a defect. Plane it at its summer size and you'll have a permanent gap the rest of the year, plus raw edge that needs sealing. The better fixes are adjusting or shimming the hinges, tightening loose hinge screws into solid material, and easing only the specific contact point if one exists. We remove material only when a door genuinely doesn't fit year-round.

Can you fix bifold doors or do they need replaced?

Most of the time the doors themselves are fine and it's the hardware that's failed — pivots, guides, and track. Replacing that hardware and re-hanging the doors properly is far cheaper than new doors and solves the problem for years. Replacement makes sense when the panels themselves are damaged, delaminating, or you want a different look. We'll tell you which situation you're in when we look at them.

Do you match existing trim profiles?

We try, and in Robinson we usually succeed, because the colonial and ranch profiles used through the 1990s and 2000s are still stocked at local suppliers. If a profile has been discontinued, we find the closest available match and show it against your existing trim before installing. Where an exact match isn't possible, running the new profile through a full room rather than patching one section is often the cleaner-looking outcome, and we'll walk you through that choice rather than deciding it for you.

Do you paint the trim after repairing it?

We finish repairs ready for paint, and we can handle the painting too as part of our interior painting and touch-ups. Worth knowing on trim specifically: touching up a repaired section with leftover paint often doesn't blend, because existing trim paint has yellowed with age while the new coat hasn't. Painting the full run — corner to corner, or door to door — is what makes it look right.

I have a list of doors, not just one. Is that a problem?

It's the ideal way to book us. Nearly all of the fixed cost in this kind of work is the trip and the setup, so handling eight doors, three closets, and a run of baseboard in one visit costs dramatically less per item than calling separately for each. Send us a list — or a few photos and a rough description — and we'll give you a flat-rate price for the whole thing. See our punch list and maintenance visits.

My storm door won't stay closed. Do you handle those?

Yes — storm and screen door work is a regular part of what we do. The usual culprits are a failed closer, a latch that's fallen out of alignment with the strike, or hinges that have loosened at the frame. All straightforward fixes. If the door itself is bent or the frame has pulled away, replacement may be the better call, and we'll be straight with you about which. See storm and screen door repair.
